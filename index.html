Week 1 Lesson Guide — Teaching Companion

Who this is for. The parent, tutor, or the student themselves (gifted students in this program are expected to grow into self-teaching). Language Arts — Diagnostic Essay (Assignment LA-1)

Objective. A baseline writing sample under test conditions.

Teach. Explain the format once: “Private-school entrance tests give you 30 minutes and one prompt. Today you get 45 because it’s your first. The graders look for three things: a clear position stated in your first paragraph, specific examples — not vague ones — and correct sentences.” Then start the timer and step away. Do not help.

Grading. Don’t assign a letter grade. Instead, together, mark: (1) Is there a thesis in paragraph 1? (2) Does each body paragraph have one clear example? (3) Circle every sentence error without fixing it — the student fixes them Tuesday as their editing warm-up. File this essay; in Week 16 the student rewrites the same prompt and compares.

Also today: hand out Vocabulary List 1. Teach the method: “Don’t memorize 20 words. Memorize 3 roots — spec means look, gnos means know, vis means see — and the 20 words become obvious. This is how you’ll beat the vocabulary sections of any entrance test: roots let you decode words you’ve never seen.” Have the student read the list aloud once, guessing how each word connects to its root before reading the definition.

Science — The Scientific Method (Worksheet S-1, part A–B)

Objective. Student can name the steps of the scientific method and identify variables and controls in a described experiment.

Teach. The core idea in one line: “Science is not a list of facts — it is a method for finding out which ideas survive testing.” Walk through one full example out loud before the worksheet — use this one: “Does a phone in the room hurt sleep quality?” Ask the student to propose: the question, a testable hypothesis (“If a phone is in the bedroom overnight, then sleep will be shorter”), what you’d change (independent variable: phone in room or not), what you’d measure (dependent variable: hours slept), and what stays the same (controlled variables: bedtime, caffeine, noise). Then define control group as “the comparison group that gets no treatment — without it, you can’t know if your treatment did anything.”

Common error. Students swap independent and dependent variables. The fix that sticks: **“I** change the Independent variable; the Dependent variable Depends on it.”

Assign. Worksheet S-1 parts A and B today; part C (design your own experiment) starts today and finishes tomorrow.

TUESDAY

Math — Lesson 1: Operations with Signed Numbers (Worksheet M-1)

Objective. Fluent addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division with negative numbers.

Teach. Use money, not rules, first: “Negative numbers are debt. −8 + 3 means you owe 8 and pay back 3: you still owe 5. −8 + (−3) means you owe 8 and borrow 3 more: you owe 11.” Then give the two formal rules: same signs → add absolute values, keep the sign; different signs → subtract, keep the sign of the bigger. For subtraction, one rule only: subtracting is adding the opposite — rewrite 8 − 15 as 8 + (−15) every time until it’s automatic. For multiplication/division: same signs positive, different signs negative; three negatives multiplied → negative (count the negative signs: odd = negative, even = positive).

Worked examples (do together before the worksheet): −6 − (−10) → −6 + 10 = 4. (−3)(−2)(−5) → two negatives make +6, times −5 → −30.

Common errors. (1) Treating −8 + 3 as −11 (adding instead of subtracting the absolute values). (2) Sign errors with double negatives — always rewrite as addition first.

Grading. Key at end of packet. Perfect score on section A → student may skip to section C tomorrow.

Language Arts — Grammar Bootcamp 1: Parts of Speech (Worksheet LA-2)

Objective. Instantly identify all eight parts of speech in real sentences.

Teach. Frame it: “Grammar is not memorizing labels — it’s learning the moving parts of a sentence so that when your writing breaks, you know how to fix it.” Review the eight parts with this test-each-word method: a noun names, a verb does or is, an adjective describes a noun, an adverb describes a verb/adjective/adverb (often -ly), a preposition starts a phrase telling where/when/how (on the shelf), a conjunction joins (and/but/or/nor/for/so/yet), a pronoun replaces a noun, an interjection is an exclamation. Key teaching point: part of speech is decided by the word’s job in that sentence, not by the word itself — “run” is a verb in “I run” and a noun in “a morning run.”

Warm-up first: the student fixes the circled sentence errors from Monday’s diagnostic essay.

Common error. Adverbs vs. adjectives (“She did good” vs. “She did well”). Also: mislabeling prepositional-phrase nouns as subjects — in problem C, the subject is explorers, never voyage or Atlantic.

WEDNESDAY

Math — Lesson 2: Order of Operations (Worksheet M-2)

Objective. Correct evaluation of multi-step expressions with grouping symbols and exponents.

Teach. “Math is a language, and order of operations is its punctuation — without it, 5 + 2 × 3 could mean 21 or 11, and mathematicians can’t have two answers.” Teach the hierarchy: grouping symbols → exponents → multiply/divide left to right → add/subtract left to right. Stress the two famous traps: multiplication does NOT come before division (they’re the same rank, left to right), and same for addition/subtraction: 18 − 6 ÷ 2 is 18 − 3 = 15, and 10 − 4 + 2 is 6 + 2 = 8, not 4.

Worked examples: 4² − (7 − 3) × 2 → 16 − 4 × 2 → 16 − 8 = 8. Nested grouping: 3[10 − (4 + 2)] → 3[10 − 6] → 3(4) = 12 (innermost first).

Common errors. Doing addition before subtraction because “A comes before S”; forgetting that a fraction bar is a grouping symbol.

Note the Saxon structure: from today on, section B of every worksheet reviews all previous topics. This is permanent. If the student asks why they’re still doing signed numbers, the answer is: “Because in this program, nothing you learn is ever allowed to fade.”

Language Arts — The Five Themes of Geography (Worksheet LA-3)

Teach. Before the passage, activate thinking: “Name our nearest big city. Now — why is it exactly THERE and not fifty miles away?” (Almost every city answer involves water, routes, or resources — that’s geography doing its work.) Then have the student read the passage once straight through, then a second time annotating: underline each theme’s name, circle its definition. After the worksheet. Discuss question 3 aloud (the “lenses” metaphor) — push past “it means different views” to why that matters: the same city looks completely different through movement than through place, and a geographer needs all five.

Writing connection. The paragraph in question 6 must have a topic sentence, at least three themes correctly applied to their own region, and specific details (names of actual mountains, rivers, industries — not “there are some mountains”).

Science — Metric Measurement Lab (Worksheet S-2)

Objective. Convert metric units fluently; measure accurately; record data in a proper table.

Teach. “Every measurement in science answers two questions: how much, and of what unit. A number without a unit is not data.” Teach the staircase: King Henry Died By Drinking Chocolate Milk (kilo–hecto–deka–base–deci–centi–milli). Each step down the staircase moves the decimal one place right; each step up, one place left. 3.5 km → m is three steps down → 3,500 m.

Common error. Moving the decimal the right number of places in the wrong direction. The sanity check: “A kilometer is huge, a meter is small — so kilometers-to-meters must make the number bigger.” Estimate before measuring in part C — the gap between estimate and measurement is itself a lesson.

THURSDAY

Math — Lesson 3: Fractions, Decimals & Percents (Worksheet M-3)

Objective. Convert fluently among fractions, decimals, and percents; compute with all three.

Teach. The unifying idea: “Fractions, decimals, and percents are three costumes on the same number. 3/4, 0.75, and 75% are not related numbers — they are the SAME number.” Conversions: fraction→decimal is divide; decimal→percent slides the decimal two right; percent→fraction is over 100, then reduce. For adding/subtracting fractions: common denominators, always. For “35% of 140”: of means multiply — 0.35 × 140. For “27 is what percent of 60”: part over whole — 27/60 = 45%.

Worked examples: 3/4 − 2/5 → 15/20 − 8/20 = 7/20. Mixed numbers: 2⅓ + 1¾ → 2 4/12 + 1 9/12 = 3 13/12 = 4 1/12.

Common errors. Adding denominators (2/3 + 1/4 ≠ 3/7 — ever); forgetting to reduce; in percent problems, dividing by the part instead of the whole.

Honors challenge tip (problem 14). “A number decreased by 40% equals 36” means x − 0.4x = 36, so 0.6x = 36. The insight worth saying aloud: decreasing by 40% is the same as keeping 60%.

Reading & Geography — The Five Themes mixed

Read the passage twice — once straight through, once annotating. Then answer. Multiple-choice questions grade themselves; written answers have a model answer to compare against.

Geographers organize their study of the world around five themes. Location answers the question "Where is it?" — either absolutely, with coordinates of latitude and longitude, or relatively, in terms of what it is near. Place answers "What is it like there?", describing physical features such as mountains and climate, and human features such as language and architecture. Human-environment interaction examines how people adapt to, modify, and depend upon their surroundings: the Dutch reclaiming farmland from the sea with dikes, or desert cities piping in water across hundreds of miles. Movement traces the flow of people, goods, and ideas — trade routes, migrations, even the spread of inventions and religions. Finally, region groups areas that share common characteristics, whether physical, like the Sahara, or cultural, like Latin America. The five themes are not five separate subjects but five lenses; pointed at any spot on Earth, together they explain not just where things are, but why they are there and why it matters.

1. Which theme would a geographer most likely use to study the Silk Road?

✔ Correct — the Silk Road is the flow of goods, people, and ideas.
✘ Answer: movement — trade routes are flows of goods, people, and ideas.

2. "Absolute location" means:

✔ Correct.
✘ Answer: exact coordinates. "What it is near" is relative location.

3. Which is an example of human-environment interaction from the passage?

✔ Correct — people modifying their surroundings.
✘ Answer: the Dutch dikes — people modifying their environment.

4. The metaphor of "five lenses" suggests the themes are:

✔ Correct — each lens reveals something the others miss.
✘ Answer: different ways of viewing the same place — pointed together at one spot.

5. Vocabulary in context: which List 1 word best describes a geographer who carefully considers all consequences before approving a dam?

✔ Correct.
✘ Answer: circumspect — cautious, weighing consequences.

6. Writing (paragraph): Using at least three of the five themes, describe the place where you live. Write it below, then compare with the model.

Model (yours will differ — check the bones, not the details): "My town sits where a river canyon opens onto a plain (location — relative). It is a high-desert place of sagebrush hills, cold winters, and ranch houses (place). Farmers here depend on irrigation canals that carry river water to dry fields, changing the land to make it grow food (human-environment interaction). Nearly everything in our stores arrives by the interstate, and many families came here from other states for work (movement)."

Checklist: a topic sentence · at least 3 themes named or clearly used · specific real details (actual rivers, crops, roads — not "some mountains").

What do you already think AI is?

1. In your own words: what is artificial intelligence?

One useful answer: "A computer system that learned patterns from huge amounts of examples, and uses those patterns to make predictions or generate answers." Notice what's NOT in that sentence: thinking, knowing, or being alive. We'll test your Week 1 answer against what you learn in Weeks 5–8.

2. List five places you met AI in the last week.

Phone autocorrect and voice assistants · streaming and video recommendations · map routing · photo face-grouping · spam filters · game opponents · chatbots. If you found five, you're already more cognizant than most adults.

3. Can an AI chatbot be wrong? Why might that happen?

Yes — often confidently wrong. It predicts likely-sounding answers from patterns; it doesn't check facts unless asked or connected to sources. Rule for this program: verify anything an AI tells you against a real source (there's your Week 8 vocabulary word).

4. Where is the line between AI helping with homework and AI doing it? Write your own rule.

A strong starting rule: "AI may explain, quiz me, and check my finished work. It may not write my sentences or solve my problems first." You'll write your official policy in Week 8 — keep this draft.
